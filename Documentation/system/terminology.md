# Terminology

#### Extracted from the Taurite Tu website

__Kaumātua & Pakeke__: A participant is referred to respectfully as a kaumātua (elder) or pakeke (adult).

__Whānau (Family)__: This means family, but in this context, it expands to the broader community. Participants don't exercise in isolation; they are encouraged to bring their whānau along. The group exercises together, supporting each other like a family unit.

__Rōpū (Group/Committee)__: The organizers, leaders, and guiding participants function as a rōpū kaiārahi (a guiding group), ensuring the program belongs to the people, not a corporate entity. 

__The Environment (The Social System) Whanaungatanga (Connection)__: This is the core practice of building close, family-like relationships. In Taurite Tū, this happens heavily during the second hour of the class. Sharing a conversation and bonding is considered just as important for a person's health as the physical movements themselves.

__Kai (Food)__: Instead of gulping down a protein shake alone, the group sits down to share kai (food). Eating together breaks down social barriers and nourishes the body in a communal way.

__Marae (Meeting Grounds)__: Hosting classes at a marae provides cultural safety. It is an environment rooted in ancestral connection, making it feel welcoming and sacred rather than clinical or intimidating.

__The Purpose (The Philosophy)Hauora (Holistic Wellbeing)__: In Western fitness, health is often just physical. Hauora encompasses four dimensions: taha tinana (physical health), taha wairua (spiritual health), taha whānau (family health), and taha hinengaro (mental health). The program targets all four.
 
__Tikanga / Tikaka (Cultural Customs & Protocols)__: The classes are guided by tikanga (proper cultural practices), such as starting with a karakia (prayer/incantation) to clear the space and ensure everyone's safety and focus.
 
__Mātauranga / Mātauraka Māori (Māori Knowledge)__: Rather than using standard physiotherapy terminology (like "lateral extensions" or "squats"), the exercises are taught through mātauranga Māori—using the stories, history, and physical arts of the culture to guide how the body moves
